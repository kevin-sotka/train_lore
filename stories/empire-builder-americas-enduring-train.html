The Empire Builder: How One Man’s Vision Created America’s Most Enduring Train

As told this week · set down by the lantern-keeper, from the Tale No. 21 of the Lore

diorama image pending — empire-builder-americas-enduring-train-hero.png

Pull your collar up and sit a spell. Most of the tales I tell you end with a thing buried, a mountain won or a fortune lost. This one ends different. This one ends with a train that is, at this very hour, rolling somewhere across the northern plains with its lamps lit, same as it has near enough every day since the tenth of June, 1929.

They called it the Empire Builder, and the name was a tip of the hat to a man. You'll know him if you've sat with me before: James J. Hill, the one-eyed Scot-Canadian who built the Great Northern clear across the top of the country without a dime of government land grant. I told you of him in The One-Eyed Empire Builder. By 1929 the old man had been in the ground thirteen years. The railroad he left behind put his nickname on its finest train and sent it west.

A Name Big Enough for the Job

The Great Northern wanted a flagship, a Chicago-to-Seattle limited that said what the road was made of. It ran by way of the Twin Cities, across the wheat and the prairie, up through Great Falls and over the Continental Divide, then down through Spokane and on to the coast. Folks along the line set their clocks by it, or claimed to.

A man's name on a railroad is a promise, and the Great Northern meant to keep this one every single day.

The crossing it made was the same crossing that John Stevens walked alone one December night to find Marias Pass, and it ran down the long grade where the mountain took Wellington and over the rails that came through the Cascade Tunnel. Every hard-won mile in my earlier tales is a mile this train rides over, easy as a Sunday drive.

Glass, Steel, and Diesel Smoke

The road did not let the Builder go stale. In 1947 the Great Northern relaunched it as a streamliner, with diesel power and lightweight cars, and the schedule dropped to about forty-five hours, some thirteen hours quicker than the heavy old steel consist. Then came the Mid-Century Builder in 1951, and in the middle fifties the Great Domes, glass-topped cars from the Budd works where a passenger could sit up under the sky and watch the Rockies slide past.

Now, that mountain country was the whole selling point. The schedule was set so a rider crossed the Rockies by daylight and rolled along the edge of Glacier National Park. The Great Northern had been busy for years promoting that park and its lodges, and it was no accident that its best train ran right past the front door. Business and scenery rode the same rails, and the road knew it.

The Train That Would Not Quit

You'd think a train like that would have gone the way of the steam engines. Plenty did. By the sixties the passengers had gone to the airplanes and the highways, and the railroads were glad to be shed of them. When Amtrak took over the nation's intercity trains on the first of May, 1971, the Empire Builder was kept. It had a faster line than the rival route through the old Northern Pacific country, and it had riders who would not let go of it.

So it runs yet. Today it leaves Chicago for the coast and splits at Spokane, one section bound for Seattle and one for Portland, which is a chore the old baked-potato road's North Coast Limited never had to manage. Around three hundred sixty thousand people ride it in a good year. Pretty good for a train named for a dead man.

What Really Happened

The Great Northern Railway launched the Empire Builder on June 10, 1929, as its Chicago to Seattle flagship, named for company founder James J. Hill, whose nickname was "The Empire Builder." It was relaunched as a diesel streamliner on February 23, 1947, and re-equipped with new trainsets in 1951; Budd-built Great Dome cars entered service in 1955. Amtrak began operating on May 1, 1971, and retained the Empire Builder. Today it runs daily between Chicago and Seattle or Portland (about 2,200 miles, roughly 45 hours), dividing at Spokane, and is timed to cross the Rockies and pass Glacier National Park in daylight. Amtrak reported 364,495 riders in fiscal year 2025.

Ridership figures vary between sources and years, and some popular accounts credit James J. Hill himself with personally lobbying Congress to create Glacier National Park (established 1910); the better documented fact is that the Great Northern heavily promoted the park and built lodges there.
