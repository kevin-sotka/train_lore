Go By Train: Portland Union Station's 130 Years as Gateway to the Northwest

As told this week · set down by the lantern-keeper, from the Tale No. 20 of the Lore

Hear the tale told

I've told you of King Street Station up in Seattle, the brick cathedral with the Venetian tower. Well, come down the line a ways and I'll show you its cousin. Portland's Union Station has stood at the head of Northwest Sixth Avenue since 1896, with a clock tower a hundred and fifty feet tall and a sign on it that every child in the city could read: Go By Train. A good many of us did.

Six Years of Bricks

The plans go back to 1885, and there was talk early on of something grander still, a design from McKim, Mead & White that would have been the biggest station in the world. Nobody could pay for it. The Northern Pacific Terminal Company took the humbler plan of Van Brunt & Howe, broke ground in 1890, and spent close to six years and about $300,000 raising a Romanesque Revival depot of brick and stone. When it was done, every railroad worth the name came in under that one roof, which was the whole point. One depot for the whole city, so a traveler didn't have to guess which door was his.

A depot is only a promise in brick: that the road goes somewhere, and that you'll be let on.

The Station at Its Busiest

In the golden years the place hummed day and night. In the late 1920s the old hall was opened up, the iron columns pulled out, Italian marble put in, and dormers cut so daylight reached the waiting benches. Trains rolled in from Seattle, from San Francisco, from the east over the Columbia River line, the same Gorge we spoke of in an earlier tale. Hundreds of men worked those platforms, and among the proudest were the Pullman porters, many of them Black men who carried the bags and made the berths and kept the whole sleeping-car world running. Portland's Black community grew up close to the rails because the rails were where the steady jobs were. Their story and the station's story are one story, and it deserves its own telling some winter night.

When the Lights Went Out

In 1948 they hung the neon on the tower, blue and gold, reading Union Station and Go By Train. For twenty-three years it burned over the city. Then in March of 1971 the big railroads handed their passenger trains over to Amtrak, and the owners switched the signs off. Imagine that: the tower still standing, the clock still ticking, and the one thing it had been telling folks to do struck dark. Fewer trains came after that, and the great hall went quiet.

But Portland wouldn't leave it be. Local rail groups passed the hat, and in 1985 the neon came on again. The city's development commission bought the station and some thirty acres of old yard in 1987, the painted ceilings were brought back, and the old depot got put to rights. It's on the National Register now, and it still works for its living: Amtrak Cascades, the Coast Starlight, and the Empire Builder all call here.

What Really Happened

Portland Union Station opened in 1896 after construction began in 1890. It was designed by Van Brunt & Howe for the Northern Pacific Terminal Company, cost roughly $300,000, and is topped by a 150-foot clock tower. A larger McKim, Mead & White scheme from the early 1880s was dropped. The station was remodeled in the late 1920s. The neon “Go By Train” and “Union Station” signs were installed in 1948, switched off in March 1971 when Amtrak took over intercity passenger service, and relit in 1985 after a local fundraising campaign. The Portland Development Commission bought the station and about 31 acres of rail yard in 1987, and the station was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1975. Amtrak Cascades, the Coast Starlight, and the Empire Builder serve it today. The role of Pullman porters and Black Portland is summarized here only briefly and should be read in dedicated sources.
